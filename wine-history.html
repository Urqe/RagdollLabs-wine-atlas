Wine before the modern cellar

A clay jar can tell you something about how wine was made. It cannot tell you exactly what an ancient glass tasted like. These three stories connect surviving evidence to choices a winemaker can still make today.

Start with a distinction: an Atlas profile describes a selected modern style, not an archaeological reconstruction. Techniques varied within every place and period below. The useful questions are which parts of the grape touched the juice, what vessel held it, and what happened before it reached the table.

Georgia: the vessel and the skins

What they did. Chemical analysis of pottery from two Neolithic sites in present-day Georgia provides evidence of grape wine around 6000–5800 BCE. The sherds establish ancient wine activity; they do not give us a complete recipe. Separately, Georgia has a documented living tradition of making and keeping wine in buried, egg-shaped earthenware qvevri. In one traditional method described by UNESCO, pressed juice and grape solids enter the vessel together.

Why, and what differs today. A buried vessel offers a place to ferment and store wine. Contact with skins, pips and sometimes stems changes extraction; with white grapes, sustained skin contact can produce amber colour and a firm, tea-like grip. Today's Georgian producers vary the contact time, grapes and handling, while many other modern cellars ferment white juice off the skins in steel or other vessels. The ancient residue does not prove that a modern qvevri method has stayed identical for eight millennia, or that every qvevri wine is amber.

Try the connection: compare Kakheti qvevri amber Rkatsiteli with Kartli Rkatsiteli. Look for texture and tannin as well as colour; the profiles represent different regional choices, not an ancient-versus-modern taste test.

Evidence: McGovern and colleagues, PNAS (2017); UNESCO's record of the living qvevri tradition (2013).

Greece: vessel, journey and table

What they did. Consider the classical Greek world of roughly the fifth to fourth centuries BCE. Ceramic amphorae could carry goods across the Mediterranean, but a jar's shape alone does not prove it held wine: some carried oil or other products. Evidence from a shipwreck near Chios shows why residue and contextual analysis matter when identifying a cargo. At a symposium, wine was commonly mixed with water in a large krater before serving. That tells us about a particular social setting, not the fermentation method of every Greek wine.

Why, and what differs today. Transport jars made trade practical, while mixing at the table changed the drink as served. Certain ancient wines or containers involved resin, but resin was never a compulsory ingredient in all Greek wine. Modern Greek regional wines may use steel, wood or other vessels; retsina is a distinct surviving resinated style, not a universal window into ancient flavour. Read a modern bottle's production details before assuming that amphora, resin and dilution belong to the same process.

Try the connection: when a label mentions an amphora, ask whether it was used for fermentation, ageing or transport. Then ask whether the wine actually had skin contact. Vessel material alone cannot answer either question.

Evidence: Metropolitan Museum of Art on symposium vessels and serving; Foley and colleagues on amphora cargo identification, Journal of Archaeological Science (2012).

Rome: pressing and the great jars

What they did. Roman wineries across the empire were diverse, but archaeological sites preserve pressing installations and large earthenware dolia used in making and storing wine. Amphorae also moved wine through distribution networks. A 2024 archaeological study compares the design of Roman dolia with Georgian qvevri to investigate what vessel shape, size and surfaces might have meant for fermentation. It is a comparative interpretation, not a preserved Roman tasting note.

Why, and what differs today. Pressing separates juice from solids; vessel choice affects the conditions under which fermentation and maturation proceed. Today's producers can control temperature, hygiene and oxygen exposure more precisely, yet some deliberately choose clay or large neutral vessels. Neither technology nor clay guarantees a better wine. Ancient texts and excavated equipment show practices, while proposed aromas from reconstructions remain hypotheses: ingredients, microbes and storage could all change the result.

Try the connection: a modern “clay-aged” label is a clue about a vessel, not a promise of Roman flavour. Ask what was pressed, how long the skins remained, and whether the vessel was lined or porous.

Evidence and interpretation: Van Limbergen and colleagues, Antiquity (2024).
